A Matrix Is a Machine

Most people meet a matrix as a box of numbers with arbitrary rules for multiplying them. It is much better thought of as a function on space itself: feed it every point in the plane and it moves all of them at once. Three figures — a shear, a rotation, and a collapse through zero — and the box of numbers never looks the same again.

  • grid, basis vectors, matrix chips — all driven by always

01

A shear: every point moves

The matrix is shown live, top-left — four numbers, one of them breathing. As k grows, every point (x, y) slides to (x + k·y, y): horizontal lines stay put, vertical lines lean, and the unit square leans into a parallelogram. Yet the determinant readout never leaves 1 — a shear changes no area. That is the kind of statement a static textbook asserts; here you can watch it be true.

02

Rotation: read the columns

Reset, and turn the plane a quarter circle. The trick to reading any transformation: the columns tell you where the basis vectors land. Watch the red i arrow — its tip writes column one into the numbers; the green j arrow writes column two. By the end the columns read (0, 1) and (−1, 0): i has become "up", j has become "left". The determinant stays 1 — rotations preserve area and orientation.

03

When det crosses zero

Now the dramatic one. The entry d shrinks toward zero: the horizontal grid lines crowd together, the space collapses onto a line — at det = 0 the whole plane has lost a dimension, and information is destroyed (the transformation can no longer be undone). Keep going and d comes out negative: the grid re-inflates, but mirror-flipped — the readout turns red because the orientation of space itself has reversed.

How this page works

There is not a single keyframed position in the whole demo. Each scene's always block builds the matrix from one clock (a smoothstep shear, a quarter turn, a shrinking d), applies it to twelve grid lines and two basis vectors, and formats the four entries with format("{:.1}", …) — the numbers you read are the numbers driving the geometry.

Sources are plain text — the language tour has the syntax, and the demo shelf has more like this.
