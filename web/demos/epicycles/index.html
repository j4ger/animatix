Circles That Draw a Square Wave

A square wave looks like the least circular shape there is — hard edges, flat top, sharp corners. It is also, exactly, a sum of circles. This walkthrough stacks them one odd harmonic at a time; every figure below is a declarative scene compiling in your browser as you read, not a video.

01

The destination

Start from the end. The curve drawing itself below is four sine waves added together — frequencies 1, 3, 5 and 7, with amplitudes shrinking as 1/k. Alone, each sine is a gentle undulation; added with the right weights they cancel into flat tops, sheer sides, and corners sharp enough to clip audio. That such a jagged thing dissolves into a handful of smooth waves is the whole of Fourier's idea, and the reason your phone can store a square-ish waveform as a few numbers.

02

One circle, one sine

A sine wave is what you get when you look at rotation from the side. A pen rides the rim of a turning circle; its height over time traces a sine. In the figure the pen is driven by an always block — one cosine for x, one sine for y, evaluated every frame — while a line ties it to the point being traced on the graph. Nothing is precomputed: stop the circle and the pen stops writing.

03

Stack the odd harmonics

Now put circles on the circle. The second circle — radius ⅓, spinning three times as fast — rides the first's rim; a third (⅕, five times) rides that; a fourth (⅐, seven times) rides the third. The tip of the last one is what draws. Each added circle flattens the tops a little more and steepens the sides, and the equation underneath lights up the term responsible. Four circles is already embarrassingly square; the residual ripple dies as 1/k.

04

Only odd, shrinking as 1/k

Why those frequencies and weights? A square wave is anti-symmetric: flip it upside down and shift half a period and you get the same wave. That symmetry kills every even harmonic, and the quiet halves of the odd ones are what sharpen the corners. Their amplitudes fall as 1/k — each term contributes a third, a fifth, a seventh of the first. That slow decay is why corners are expensive to store: sharp edges live in the high-frequency terms a lossy codec throws away first.

05

Why circles at all?

Because rotation is the atom of periodic motion. Writing a wave as circles is the same as writing it as complex exponentials — e^(iθ) is a point on the unit circle, and each term in the series is one such point spinning at k times the base rate with radius a_k. Fourier analysis, the DFT in your audio codec, the JPEG in your image library: all of them are this picture, dressed differently. Sum enough circles and even a square wave is just rotation.

How this page works

There is no video and no backend. The hero figure plays one .amx file containing all five scenes joined by play … [fade, 500ms] transitions; the five section figures are single-scene extracts of the same source. The pen, the epicycle chain and the complex-plane vector are not keyframed — an always block evaluates their positions every frame from one clock.

Every scene source is plain text — open any .amx ↗ link above, or take the language tour to see how they are built.
