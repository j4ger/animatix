The Transformer Architecture, Animated

Every language model you have used — GPT, Claude, Llama — is built from one machine, repeated: the transformer block. This walkthrough builds it up piece by piece, and every figure is not a video but a declarative scene file, compiled in your browser and drawn with WebGPU as you read.

Start with the map below. The gold numbers on it (01–06) are the sections of this article; each section zooms into one tagged part of the diagram. If you only remember one thing: tokens flow up. Text goes in at the bottom as symbols, climbs through a stack of identical blocks — each one mixing information between tokens and then transforming each token on its own — and arrives at the top as a vector rich enough to score every word in the vocabulary.

A note on scope before we start. Everything below describes the shape of the machine — what each piece is for and how data moves through it — with the real numbers from GPT-2-size models as ballast. It will not tell you how the weights were learned, and it skips the variants (encoder–decoders, MoE, linear attention) entirely; the recap in §07 lists exactly what was left out. With that said, the shape has been remarkably stable since 2017: scale the widths, swap a few normalizations, and the block in the figure is the block that answers your questions.

01

From text to tokens

A transformer never sees words. The first thing that happens to your text is tokenization: a fixed, learned vocabulary of sub-word pieces splits the string into tokens — whole short words, syllables of longer ones, punctuation, spaces. unbelievable might come out as un · believ · able; a rare name as three or four fragments. Byte-level schemes like GPT-2's go further and make the vocabulary total: any string, in any language or no language at all, can be split — there is no “unknown word”.

The vocabulary is learned, not designed: byte-pair encoding starts from single characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent pair until the budget — 50,257 entries for GPT-2 — is spent. The merges follow the training corpus, which is why English prose tends to cost fewer tokens per word than code or German: common spellings got merged first. It is also why models are oddly bad at counting letters or doing character-level puns — by the time the model sees your question, the individual letters may not exist anywhere in its input.

Each token id then buys one thing: a row in a giant learned table called the embedding matrix. Row 4132 of the table is just d_model numbers — for GPT-2, 768 of them — and those numbers are the token as far as the rest of the network is concerned. Similar words end up with similar vectors, because that is what training rewards, but nothing about a vector is readable: the figure below shows five of the paper's 512 dimensions, where brighter simply means a larger number. From here on, every operation in this article is arithmetic on these lists. The words never come back until the very end.

  • GPT-2 vocabulary 50,257 tokens
  • d_model 768 (paper: 512)
  • embedding table alone 38.6 M parameters

How the figure unfolds

  1. 1The text, as one string — all the model will ever get.
  2. 2The tokenizer cuts it into chips. The cut points are learned statistics, not dictionary words.
  3. 3Each chip looks up its row in the embedding table and from here on is that column of numbers. The bracket names its length: d_model.
02

Positional encoding

Here is an uncomfortable property of the machine so far: it has no sense of order. Attention — the operation coming next — treats its input as an unordered bag of vectors: shuffle dog bites man into man bites dog and every intermediate computation shuffles with it, producing exactly the correspondingly-shuffled result. Word order is most of the meaning of a sentence, so it has to be put back by hand: before anything else, each position is stamped with a fingerprint vector, and the token's embedding is replaced by embedding + position.

The original transformer builds each fingerprint out of sines of geometrically growing wavelength, in sin/cos pairs. Dimension 0 oscillates fast; each later pair is a slower sine; by the last dimensions the wave barely turns over thousands of positions. Read across, every position gets a unique code; read down, the pattern is smooth, so “position 5 relative to position 4” looks the same everywhere in the text — a shift in position is a shift of the wave, and the model can learn to detect offsets rather than absolute spots. Move a word and its whole input vector changes, even though its embedding did not.

The field has since tried three answers to the same problem, and it is worth naming them because they keep trading places: learned absolute vectors (GPT-2 — one free parameter per position, simple but capped at the trained length), sinusoids (the paper — free parameters saved, extrapolates further), and rotary embeddings (RoPE) — the current default in Llama-class models — which abandons the stamp-adding picture and instead rotates the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to position inside attention, so a dot product between two tokens depends only on their distance. Whatever the scheme, the figure's point stands: order must be injected, explicitly, at the bottom of the stack.

  • sine wavelengths 2π → 10000·2π
  • GPT-2: learned position vectors instead
  • modern Llama-style models: RoPE, applied inside attention

How the figure unfolds

  1. 1The matrix sweeps in: one fingerprint per position, four dimensions shown of d_model.
  2. 2The row labels tell the mechanism: period 3, 6, 12, 24 — every row oscillates half as fast. (Schematic: the real constants span thousands of positions.)
  3. 3The cursor walks the columns. One column, read top to bottom, is the complete stamp added to one token — dark is −1, bright is +1.
03

Self-attention

This is the engine of the whole architecture. Each token's vector is projected three ways, through three learned matrices, into three roles. Its query is the question it is asking — “I'm a pronoun; who do I refer to?”. Its key is what it advertises — “I'm a noun, an animal, singular”. Its value is the content it hands over if someone attends to it. Every token's query is scored against every token's key with a dot product; the scores go through a softmax, which turns them into positive weights that sum to exactly 1; and the token's new vector is the weighted average of everyone's values.

The softmax is worth feeling concretely, because so much of attention's behaviour hides in it. Suppose it's dot products come out as 14.0 against animal, 11.5 against itself, and 9.0-ish against everything else. Dividing by √d_k (with d_k = 64, divide by 8) brings them to roughly 1.75, 1.44, 1.1; the exponential makes 5.75, 4.22, 3.0; normalizing lands on 0.43, 0.32, 0.22. Two properties fall out for free: the gaps dominate (small score differences grow after the exponential), and the weights always sum to exactly 1 no matter how long the sentence gets — a longer context dilutes every weight rather than growing the total. When a model “loses track” over very long inputs, it is often this normalization you are watching: one token's fixed attention budget spread across too many candidates.

In the sentence below, it puts most of its weight — 0.62 — on animal: that is coreference being resolved by arithmetic. One ingredient is deliberately not drawn: a decoder also applies a causal mask, forbidding every query from scoring keys at later positions (the future must stay unseen while generating). Attention is the only step in the whole block where tokens exchange information; everything else happens to each position independently.

  • scores = q·k / √d_k, then softmax
  • d_k = 64 → divisor 8
  • weights sum to 1; output = Σ w·value
  • decoders: a causal mask hides the future

How the figure unfolds

  1. 1The ring marks the query: it looks back over everything so far.
  2. 2Every token is scored — the bars are those compatibilities, one per token, on one baseline.
  3. 3Softmax turns scores into weights that sum to 1; the printed numbers show how lopsided it usually is. The arrow is the mixture actually taken.
04

Multi-head attention

One attention pattern is one story per token, and language needs several at once — who did what to whom, which pronoun binds where, what sits next to what. So the model doesn't run attention once over d_model-dimensional vectors; it runs h heads in parallel, each working in a small slice of the space and each with its own Q/K/V projections. Nothing forces the heads to specialize, but trained heads reliably do: this head tracks subjects and verbs, that one references, another just keeps neighbouring tokens together. The most famous of them, induction heads, implement an abstract “find where this pattern occurred before, copy what came next” — the circuit that powers in-context learning.

The budget is fixed: h heads × d_k dimensions per head = d_model, so more heads means narrower heads. Twelve heads of 64 (GPT-2 small) and four heads of 192 do the same amount of arithmetic — the choice is how many relations to track at once versus how much detail each relation gets. The figure shows three heads' patterns over the same three tokens — rows asking, columns answering, brightness the weight. Different bright cells: different learned relations over identical input.

Afterwards the head outputs — each short, d_model/h dimensional — are concatenated back to full width and pushed through one final learned matrix, the output projection WO, which lets heads combine: the grammar head's finding gets mixed with the reference head's. The three-coloured bar at the bottom of the figure is the concatenation; the “linear mix” caption is WO doing the fusing.

  • GPT-2 small: 12 heads × 64 dims
  • h × d_k = d_model: the budget is fixed
  • concat → one output matrix WO mixes them

How the figure unfolds

  1. 1Three empty panels: the same tokens, about to be read three ways.
  2. 2The heads fill in one after another, each a 3×3 pattern — rows are queries, columns keys. The pulses point at each head's specialty: subject↔verb, verb→object, next-token.
  3. 3Outputs drop into the bar below, one coloured segment per head: concatenated. The caption's “linear mix” is the projection that lets the heads' findings combine.
05

Feed-forward & residuals

Attention moves information between positions; the feed-forward network (FFN) then works on each position alone, applying the same two-layer MLP everywhere: widen the vector to 4×d_model, pass through a nonlinearity (GELU), project back down. The widening is the point — in that inflated space the model keeps what is essentially a huge associative memory. One way researchers read it: the widened layer's neurons are pattern detectors (keys), and the projection back writes the matching entries' contributions (values) into the vector — “running” + sports context activates sprint-ish features and deactivates desk-ish ones. Facts live here, per token.

The other protagonist in this figure is the line underneath: the residual stream. Every sublayer — attention, FFN — does not replace the flowing vector but adds to it: x ← x + F(LN(x)), with the arcs in the figure showing exactly what is being added on top of the rail. The additions are what let a hundred layers train stably: the original signal is always one skip away, so each block only has to learn a small refinement, and gradients have a highway to the input. The small LN rings are layer normalization — a per-vector rescale that keeps the stream's magnitude steady — read from the stream just before each block.

Count parameters and the FFN stops looking like a detail. One block holds four d_model×d_model attention matrices (4d²) plus two FFN matrices of d_model×4d_model (8d²) — the FFN is two thirds of every block, and about that share of a whole model. It is also where most of the interesting recent swaps happen: GELU → SwiGLU (a gated variant that trades the neat 4× for a better parameter/quality curve), dense → mixture-of-experts (many FFNs, a router picks one or two per token — the architecture doubles but the compute per token stays flat). When people say a model “has 70B parameters”, most of that number lives in the boxes on the right.

  • d_model → 4×d_model → d_model
  • per block: attention 4d² + FFN 8d² params
  • every sublayer: x + F(LN(x))
  • modern swaps: SwiGLU, MoE

How the figure unfolds

  1. 1The rail draws in: the stream the token's vector travels in, passing under everything that follows.
  2. 2The first half-block appears — normalize, attend — then the second: LN, FFN. The bars inside FFN show the widening to 4× and the projection back.
  3. 3The skip arcs complete and the + marks flash: nothing was overwritten, only added. The dot then rides the rail past both blocks — the original signal, still intact.
06

Stacking the blocks

One attention-plus-FFN block is a layer. The full model is N of them in series — 12 in GPT-2 small, 96 in GPT-3 — each with its own weights: the blocks are structurally identical, but block 7 is not block 3 reused. The input to the stack is a row of position-stamped embedding vectors; the output is the same row, same length, but every vector has been rewritten N times — mixed with its neighbours N times, refined through N memories. The figure's little intensity strip inside each block is that state: same tokens in, different meaning out.

What do deeper layers actually do? Probing work suggests a rough gradient: early layers handle local, surface patterns; middle layers pick up syntax and facts; late layers sharpen into prediction-shaped features. Nothing enforces that division — it falls out of training. The residual-stream view makes depth easier to reason about: the stream is shared workspace, and each of the 2N sublayers just reads from it and adds a contribution. From that angle, “how deep” is “how many sequential refinement steps the model can afford” — and the answer has mostly been set by compute budgets, not by theory.

At the top sits the LM head: one final projection from d_model up to the vocabulary size, one logit per token, and a softmax over all of them. Pick a token from that distribution — greedily, or with the temperature/top-p sampling you have seen in every chat UI — append it to the input, run the whole stack again: the loop in the opening figure. Because each new token attends to everything before it, the model re-reads the whole context every single token; the KV cache (remembering each token's keys and values so they are not recomputed) is the engineering that makes that affordable. Training is this same forward pass over trillions of words, with the loss simply measuring how often the picked token was the true next one.

  • GPT-2 small: 12 layers · GPT-3: 96
  • each layer has its own weights
  • output row = input row, contextualized
  • LM head: d_model → 50,257 logits

How the figure unfolds

  1. 1A sentence becomes chips; its vectors enter the stack at the bottom.
  2. 2The blocks stack up one by one, each stamped with a state strip. Identical boxes — but each layer owns its own weights.
  3. 3At the top the final vector fans out to heads: LM head scores every token in the vocabulary; a task head would read the same vector for classification. The climbing dot is one token's whole journey.
07

Recap, glossary & reading

That is the whole machine. One pass through a transformer, in order:

  1. Tokenize — text becomes a list of vocabulary ids.
  2. Embed — each id looks up a d_model vector.
  3. Add position — fingerprints restore word order.
  4. Repeat N times — each block: attention (tokens mix), then FFN (each token transforms), both wrapped in x + F(LN(x)).
  5. LM head — the final vectors become vocabulary logits.
  6. Softmax & sample — pick the next token, append it, go again.

Glossary

termmeaning
tokenone piece of text from the learned vocabulary — a word fragment, symbol, or space
embeddingthe d_model-number vector a token's row in the lookup table holds
d_modelthe width of the main highway; 512 in the paper, 768 in GPT-2 small
positional encodingthe fingerprint added to each embedding so order is visible
query / key / valuethe three projections each token wears in attention: what it seeks, what it advertises, what it gives
attention weighta softmaxed score — how much of one token's value another token takes (all weights sum to 1)
headone attention operation in a narrow slice of d_model; many run in parallel
FFN / MLPthe per-token two-layer network: d_model → 4·d_model → d_model with GELU
residual streamthe vector that flows up the stack; every sublayer adds its contribution rather than replacing it
LayerNorm (LN)a per-vector rescale that keeps the stream's magnitude steady
LM headthe final projection to one logit per vocabulary entry
softmaxturns any list of scores into positive weights summing to 1
autoregressivegenerating by repeatedly appending your own predicted next token

What this article left out

Training and backpropagation; the encoder–decoder variant and cross-attention (machine translation's original shape); sampling knobs like temperature and top-p, beyond a mention; the KV cache's actual bookkeeping; long-context tricks (RoPE scaling, sliding windows, attention sinks); grouped-query attention and its relatives; mixture-of-experts routing, beyond a mention; multimodal encoders; and scaling laws. None of them change the picture above; they change its parts.

How this page works

There is no video here and no backend. Each figure loads a .amx text file at runtime, compiles it in the browser, and renders it with WebGPU. All seven share one engine download and one GPU device, and a figure that scrolls out of view pauses itself. 

<script type="module" src="../../embed/amx-player.js"
        data-runtime-base="../../pkg-slim"></script>

<amx-player src="scenes/attention.amx" autoplay loop controls
            hold="1.5"></amx-player>

The scenes use plain text only, so the page serves the playback-only pkg-slim profile instead of the full engine — about 1.6 MB brotli (5 MB raw on hosts without brotli, like GitHub Pages) against 29 MB. The full profile is only needed for Typst markup, equations and image or SVG assets.

The scenes also share a small component library: the token chips on this page are all instances of one LabeledBox component from the shared components.amx library (examples/lib/components.amx, served beside these scenes as lib/components.amx), imported by every scene that needs a box-with-a-label. The scenes themselves are written against layout containers (Row/Col/Grid) and keyframe assignments rather than hand-computed positions — see the .amx ↗ sources.

hold is the pause before a looping figure restarts. A scene's timeline ends at its last keyframe, so without a hold the finished diagram would cut straight back to an empty stage; the player rests the finished frame, then dissolves it out and back in.

The scene sources are plain text — click any .amx ↗ link above to read one, or open the QA page.
