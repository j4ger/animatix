Rolling Downhill

Training a neural network is often described as "the model learns". Mechanically, it is a ball rolling downhill on a loss surface — and the only question is which way is down. This walkthrough builds that intuition in four figures: the surface, the direction, the roll, and the step that decides whether the ball ever arrives.

01

The loss surface

A model has parameters (the knobs), and a loss function that scores how wrong it is for every setting of those knobs. Two parameters give a two-dimensional surface you can draw: here f(x, y) = x² + y², a bowl. The rings are contours — everywhere the loss is equal — and the grey arrows are the gradient, pointing uphill at every point. The green dot at the center is where the model would be perfect.

02

Go against the gradient

The gradient points uphill — the direction of steepest increase. Training wants the opposite: from wherever the ball sits, step against the gradient and the loss strictly decreases. On this bowl the gradient is radial, so "downhill" is always a straight line toward the green minimum. Real loss surfaces are not bowls — they have ridges, valleys and flat plateaus where the gradient lies about the way out — but the rule never changes.

03

Roll to the minimum

Put the two together and train: step against the gradient, re-read it, step again. The ball's trail is a PlotCurve tracing a parametric spiral; its position and the live loss readout are driven by an always block — the same expression evaluated every frame, with descent_graph.map(x, y) converting data coordinates to screen coordinates. Loss 14.75 → 0.10 is the whole of training, miniature.

04

Step size matters

One knob remains, and it is the one practitioners actually tune: the learning rate — how far each step travels. Too small, and training crawls (grey). Too large, and every step overshoots the valley and climbs the far wall — the loss diverges (red). Just right, and the ball settles (green). Everything else in modern optimisation — momentum, Adam, schedules — is engineering around this one dial.

How this page works

The surface, field and trails are all drawn from functions — ContourSet, VectorField and PlotCurve take closures, not data. The rolling ball is an always block with graph.map() doing the data-to-screen conversion, and the loss readout is format("loss = {:.2}", …) updating a text actor every frame.
